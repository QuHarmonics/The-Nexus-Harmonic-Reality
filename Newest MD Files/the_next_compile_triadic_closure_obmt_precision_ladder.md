## 13. The Address-Rigidity Problem

The next compile is therefore not another philosophical expansion.  
It is an adversarial scan.

For a target observable $X$, define the predicted value from row $r$ as:

$$
\widehat{X}(r).
$$

Define the relative residual:

$$
\delta_X(r)
=
\frac{|\widehat{X}(r)-X_{\text{obs}}|}{|X_{\text{obs}}|}.
$$

Then define the admissible set at tolerance $\tau$ by:

$$
A_X(\tau)
=
\{r : \delta_X(r) \le \tau\}.
$$

A row assignment is **rigid** only if:

1. the admissible set is small,
2. the proposed row is either unique or near-unique,
3. and the same projection family works across multiple targets without retuning.

This gives a concrete sparsity score:

$$
S_X(\tau)=|A_X(\tau)|.
$$

Small $S_X(\tau)$ means rigidity.  
Large $S_X(\tau)$ means the address map is dense and decorative.

A local uniqueness gap can also be defined:

$$
U_X = \delta_X(r_2) - \delta_X(r_1),
$$

where $r_1$ is the best-fit row and $r_2$ is the second-best row.

Large $U_X$ means an isolated minimum.  
Small $U_X$ means many near-equal fits.

This is the right quantitative object for the next compile.

## 15. The Noise-Floor Problem

To know whether the precision ladder is signal, a background rate must be established.

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a family of admissible transcendental expressions of comparable complexity.

Then define the noise floor for target $X$ at tolerance $\tau$ as:

$$
N_X(\tau)
=
\left|
\left\{
f \in \mathcal{F} :
\frac{|f-X_{\text{obs}}|}{|X_{\text{obs}}|} \le \tau
\right\}
\right|.
$$

If $N_X(\tau)$ is large, then a close expression is cheap.  
If $N_X(\tau)$ is small, then a close expression is expensive.

This is the missing adversarial baseline.

Without a noise floor, every precision hit remains underdetermined between:

- **receipt** and
- **decorated coincidence**.
