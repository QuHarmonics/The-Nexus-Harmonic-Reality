Executive Summary
In the landscape of contemporary linguistics and computational modeling, the distinction between
noun and verb—the "gap" that separates the static object from the dynamic event—has emerged
not merely as a category error but as a fundamental topological divergence. This report,
synthesizing findings from over one hundred disparate research artifacts ranging from 2024 to
2025, posits a radical re-evaluation of syntactic theory and artificial intelligence architecture. We
argue that the traditional insistence on deep, recursive hierarchy for all linguistic structures must
be "recused"—challenged and partially set aside—in favor of a dual-process model that
acknowledges the "flatness" of verbal networks, the geometric linearity of specific syntactic
dependencies, and the emergent nature of categories that arise from "different sides" of
modification.

1. The Ontology of the Gap: Theoretical and Philosophical Foundations
The inquiry into the nature of the noun and the verb is as old as the analysis of thought itself, yet
recent scholarship has reinvigorated this ancient debate with fresh data from low-resource
languages, computational error analysis, and cognitive neuroscience. The "gap" between these
two fundamental categories is not merely a grammatical convenience; it is a fissure that runs
through the very bedrock of how meaning is constructed, stored, and retrieved.
1.1 The Aristotelian Legacy and the Definition of Sides
To understand the modern computational "gap," one must first revisit the foundational definitions
that continue to haunt current annotation schemas. The investigation reveals a persistent echo of
Aristotle’s On Interpretation, which first established the boundary lines. Aristotle defined the noun
and verb not just by their syntactic function, but by their semantic completeness. A noun, he
argued, makes complete sense on its own, whereas a verb is inherently incomplete, demanding a
temporal and relational context.
1
This ancient distinction prefigures the modern "flat" versus "hierarchical" debate. The noun, self-
contained, builds hierarchies (taxonomies of animal > mammal > dog). The verb, dependent and
relational, builds "flat" networks of valency (who did what to whom). The report finds that this
philosophical duality is mirrored in the "different sides" of the brain debate, where the holistic,
pictorial nature of text (the noun-like stability) is contrasted with the linear, temporal progression
of speech and music (the verb-like flow).
1
The "gap" is further illuminated by the analogy of the "sharp or flat" in music. Just as a flat note
alters the harmonic context without changing the fundamental nature of the score as a visual
object, the shift between noun and verb often involves a subtle "flattening" or "sharpening" of
perspective rather than a total transformation of substance.
1
This metaphor is crucial for
understanding the "different sides" phenomenon in computational parsing, where the same
lexical item (e.g., invest) can slide across the gap depending on which side it is modified from.

1.2 The Embryological Metaphor: Holism vs. Reductionism
A striking and unexpected parallel emerges from the literature on developmental biology, which
provides a rigorous biological analog to the linguistic "gap." The research highlights that the
meaning of a noun or verb is limited by the entire sentence, just as the phenotype of a cell is
limited by its interactions within the entire embryo.
3
This comparison challenges the reductionist approach often taken in computational linguistics,
where words are treated as discrete atoms with fixed categorical tags. Instead, the "wholist
organicism" of embryology suggests that the "gap" between noun and verb is an artifact of
dissection. In the living sentence (or the living embryo), there is no sharp boundary, but rather a
gradient of potentiality. The "flat epithelial layer" of the eye imaginal disc, where interactions are
confined to neighboring cells in the same plane, offers a perfect biological model for "flat syntax"
languages, where grammatical relations are determined by local adjacency rather than deep
hierarchical embedding.
3
This biological perspective frames the "recusal" of hierarchy not as a surrender to disorder, but as
an embrace of "organic" interaction. If the biological substrate of form (the embryo) relies on
"flat" neighbor-interactions to generate complex structures, perhaps the linguistic substrate
(syntax) operates on similar principles, generating the illusion of hierarchy from fundamentally
flat, local constraints.
1.3 The Literary "One": Things in Motion
Before delving into the hard data of computational linguistics, it is essential to acknowledge the
"gap" as perceived by those who manipulate language most skillfully: the poets. The research
uncovers a critical insight from literary theory, specifically in the analysis of Pound and Blackmur.
The poetic eye "sees noun and verb as one: things in motion, motion in things".
4
This collapse of the gap—the "Janus-faced duality" where the machine and the self see "different
sides" of the same face—is not just poetic license. It describes the exact problem faced by
modern dependency parsers. When a parser encounters a word like invest in Chinese, it is
essentially looking at a "thing in motion." Is it the "thing" (noun/investment) or the "motion"
(verb/investing)? The error analysis shows that the parser often fails because it tries to force this
"motion-thing" into a binary box (Noun OR Verb), whereas the poetic (and perhaps cognitive)
reality is that it is both simultaneously, until collapsed by an external constraint.

2. The Geometry of Syntax: Flatness, Hierarchy, and the Linear Spine
The central conflict in the reviewed literature is geometrical: Does language inhabit a "flat"
Euclidean plane, or does it construct a "curved" Hyperbolic space? The answer, it appears,
depends entirely on whether one is looking at the noun or the verb.
2.1 The "Flat" Syntax of Non-Configurational Languages
Theoretical linguistics has long struggled to reconcile the strict, tree-based hierarchies of
Generative Grammar with the "flat," free-word-order structures of languages like Dyirbal,
Malayalam, and Warlpiri. The research confirms that in these languages, the "gap" between noun
and verb is often weakly defined or nonexistent, leading to a "flat syntax" that defies traditional
branching analysis.
5
In Role and Reference Grammar (RRG), this "flatness" is not a lack of structure but a different kind
of structure—one based on the "layered structure of the clause" rather than the recursive
embedding of phrases. The data suggests that strongly verb-initial languages are statistically
more likely to lack a sharp differentiation between noun and verb.
5
This supports the hypothesis
that "hierarchy" is a feature of noun-heavy, configurational languages (like English), while
"flatness" is a feature of verb-heavy, relational languages.
The "recusal" of the tree structure is further supported by evidence from Alsatian and German
dialects. Annotation schemas for these languages have adopted explicit flat tags (e.g., flat:name)
to handle personal names and titles.
7
This is an admission that within certain domains of language,
the recursive hypothesis fails; a name is not a tree of "Title modifying First Name modifying Last
Name," but a flat, linear string of identifiers. The "gap" here is the difference between the
linguistic theory (which wants a tree) and the linguistic reality (which is a flat line).

3. The Noun-Verb Dichotomy in Cognition and Neuroscience
If the "gap" causes computational friction in machines, it appears to be a defining feature of
biological neural architecture. The report synthesizes data from neuroscience, aphasiology, and
acquisition studies to map the topography of the noun and verb in the human brain.
3.1 Neural Topography: The "Different Sides" of the Brain
The literature presents a nuanced view of lateralization. While the popular "left brain/right brain"
dichotomy is often oversimplified, the research confirms that noun and verb processing do indeed
rely on distinct neural substrates, often described as engaging "different sides" of the cortical
network.
1
A pivotal 2025 study using Magnetoencephalography (MEG) mapped the predictive coding of
nouns and verbs. By analyzing the "flat" (zero variance) sensors against active channels,
researchers identified distinct Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) for nouns and verbs.
10
The study
concentrated on left frontal channels (A229, A212, A178) and found that the brain generates
different "prediction error" signals for the two categories.
This suggests that the brain treats nouns and verbs as fundamentally different statistical objects.
The "gap" here is a difference in predictive mechanism:
●
Nouns are predicted as static objects within a semantic field.
●
Verbs are predicted as dynamic operators that generate syntactic frames.
When these mechanisms fail, as in aphasia, the "gap" becomes a chasm. Patients often retain
access to one category while losing the other, effectively finding themselves stranded on one
"side" of the linguistic divide. Therapy is described as reassembling the "flat pieces of the
puzzle"—trying to reconnect the dissociated noun (object) with the verb (action) to reconstruct
the communicative event.

3.2 The Acquisition Gap: DLD and the Problem of Inference
The "gap" is also developmental. Research into Developmental Language Disorder (DLD) has
isolated a specific "verb gap." Children with DLD show a significantly larger deficit in verb learning
than in noun learning.
11
Why is the gap larger for verbs? The hypothesis supported by the data is that verb learning
requires inferencing from a "flat" context. To learn a noun (e.g., "cup"), a child can often rely on
direct reference (pointing). To learn a verb (e.g., "giving"), the child must process the entire
scene—the agent, the recipient, the object, and the transfer. This requires integrating information
from "different sides" of the event.
11
The "limited linguistic knowledge base" of DLD children impedes this inferencing process. They
struggle to bridge the gap between the linear stream of speech and the complex relational
structure of the verb. This confirms the "flatness" of the verb network—it is a web of relations that
must be traversed simultaneously, creating a higher cognitive load than the hierarchical lookup of
a noun.

(Taxonomies) with short average path lengths within clusters but clear boundaries between
them.

●
Verb Networks are "flat," "diffuse," and less modular. They form a giant, interconnected

4.1 The COGS Discovery: Flat Pattern Matching
One of the most provocative findings comes from the analysis of the COGS and ReCOGS
benchmarks, which test a model's ability to generalize compositionally (e.g., understanding "The
hedgehog eats" after learning "The hedgehog" and "eats" separately).
The prevailing assumption (the "Kim and Linzen" hypothesis) was that to solve these tasks, a
model must internalize a hierarchical, tree-structured grammar. However, recent findings
explicitly "recuse" this assumption. The data shows that Transformers can achieve high accuracy
on these tasks using "flat pattern matching with masking".
14
Instead of building a recursive tree, the model learns linear templates (e.g., Noun... Verb... Noun)
and applies them with a masking strategy that ignores the recursive depth. This "flat" solution is
sufficient for the benchmark, but it creates a "generalization gap" when the recursion depth
exceeds the training window.
Discovery: The "gap" in AI generalization is not necessarily a failure to learn hierarchy, but a
success in finding a "flat" shortcut that works for 99% of cases. The models have "recused" the
complex tree in favor of the efficient line. This has profound implications: perhaps human
language processing is also "flatter" than Chomskyan theory predicts, relying on pattern matching
for the bulk of communication and reserving recursion only for specific, complex "edge cases."

To bridge the gap between flat data and hierarchical meaning, computational linguists are
experimenting with the geometry of the embedding space itself.
●

●

Poincaré disk) is the mathematically optimal space for embedding trees.

The "gap" is literally a curvature of space. When we force a noun hierarchy into a flat Euclidean

hierarchy unfolds naturally. The "discovery" here is that a unified model of language may need to
be multi-manifold: using hyperbolic sub-spaces for noun taxonomies and flat Euclidean sub-

4.3 The "Flat" Policy in Robotics
In the domain of Embodied AI, the noun-verb gap translates into the command-action gap. "Flat"
policies are those that map a single high-level linguistic command ("Bag the groceries") directly to
a sequence of motor actions.
17
The research compares these "flat" policies to hierarchical ones (which break the command
down: "Pick up apple" -> "Place in bag"). The discovery is that "flat" policies suffer from a "binding
gap." They struggle to correctly bind the noun (apple) to the verb (pick) when the scene is
cluttered or novel. The "flat" command compresses too much information, losing the structural
relationships that a hierarchical planner preserves.
However, the "flat" policies are faster and more robust to noise in simple tasks. This mirrors the
cognitive findings: "flat" processing is efficient for routine, associative tasks, while "hierarchical"
processing is required for complex, novel composition.

We must "recuse" the hierarchy.
The evidence—from the "flat pattern matching" of Transformers to the "flat networks" of the
mental lexicon—suggests that a significant portion of language processing occurs on a "flat,"
relational plane. The "gap" is not traversed by climbing a tree, but by navigating a network.
