2.7 Collapsed View vs. Orthogonal View: Stack-as-Identity and Process-as-Transition
Throughout the above exposition, we have implicitly danced between two complementary perspectives on recursive
systems: the collapsed view, in which a complex stack of operations is treated as one composite identity or object; and
the orthogonal (abstract machine) view, in which we focus on the transitions and intermediate states as first-class
entities. The Nexus framework emphasizes that a deep understanding comes from weaving together both views, much
like using two eyes (with slightly different viewpoints) gives depth perception (parallax) about an object. Let us clarify
these perspectives and why balancing them is crucial:
 In the collapsed (stack-as-identity) view, all the layers of recursion are conceptually “folded up” into a single
entity. One treats a multi-step process as if it were a black-box function or a monolithic object. For example, in
the collapsed view, one might say “this neural network is an approximator F(x)F(x) that gives output yy” without
concern for its internal layers; or “the hash function H(M)H(M) outputs a digest” without tracking the 64 rounds
separately. Collapsed view is concerned with what a system does (the overall mapping from input to output, or
the overall equilibrium state of a complex system) rather than how each part flows. It aligns with the idea of
stack-as-identity: if you have a recursive call stack (like function calls within function calls), the collapsed view
sees the final result of the top-level call as the identity of the whole execution – essentially glossing over the
nested identities of subcalls. This perspective is powerful for recognizing invariants and end-to-end behaviors.
For instance, from a collapsed view, we might recognize that a certain recursive procedure computes the----------- Page42 ------------

the lattice and equilibrium concepts we discussed appear naturally in the collapsed view (e.g., H=0.35H=0.35 is a

 In the orthogonal (process-as-transition) view, we attend to the sequence of states and operations, each

in time. The orthogonal view is an “abstract machine” perspective: it treats the system as a state machine or a
flow of transformations. For example, in this view we don’t just see input MM and output hash H(M)H(M); we
see the state after round 1, round 2, ..., round 64, and how each depends on the previous. We see not just the
final proof of a theorem, but the sequence of lemmas and inferences that got there. This view aligns with
process-as-transition: every recursive call or loop iteration is a transition to a new state, and none of those
should be ignored if we want to understand dynamics. This perspective is crucial for identifying how a result
comes to be, diagnosing issues, and finding alternate routes (like orthogonal exits). It is essentially the
“microscopic” or algorithmic viewpoint. In physical terms, it’s like tracking a particle’s entire trajectory vs. just
knowing it went from point A to B. Many of the concepts like orthogonal exit, PSREQ cycles, echo power, etc.,
manifest in the orthogonal view – they pertain to what happens in between the start and finish.
The Mark1 lens that the framework speaks of is essentially an attempt to fuse these two: Mark1 sees the system both as
a whole (with overall harmonic identity) and as a layered process (with Samson’s Law regulating step-by-step). One way
Mark1 accomplishes this is by imposing self-similarity: the process mirrors the whole. For instance, the harmonic ratio

one, sometimes as many parts, and comparing.
Why is this interplay important? Because emergent structure (the “children” or artifacts of the invisible structure) often

view but hard to see in the nitty-gritty of the process. The Nexus thesis is that many puzzles in science (like
understanding consciousness, or proving deep mathematical conjectures) have eluded solution because researchers
