This report introduces the Semantic Field, a novel computational and theoretical framework that re-conceptualizes
meaning not as a static property of symbols but as a dynamic process of interaction within a structured topological

Section 1: Foundational Principles of the Semantic Field

static, high-dimensional vectors. This report proposes a fundamental shift from this representationalist view to an
interactionist one: meaning is not a stored property but a dynamic process that unfolds when a symbolic entity interacts
with a structured semantic environment. This section establishes the foundational principles of this new framework,
defining its core components and the physical analogies that provide its mathematical and conceptual machinery.

 The Set: This is the foundational structure of the framework, formally defined as a high-dimensional, structured

stage upon which meaning unfolds.
 The Glyph: In this framework, a symbol such as a word or query is not a static vector but a glyph. A glyph is

fields in physics, the Semantic Field exerts "forces" on any glyph introduced into it, guiding its interpretation
along paths of semantic coherence and away from regions of contradiction.

Section 2: The Set: Characterizing Semantic Space as a Riemannian Manifold
The geometric foundation of the Semantic Field is The Set. This section provides a formal mathematical characterization

2.3 Formalizing The Set as a Riemannian Manifold
To fully capture the rich structure of semantic space, it is necessary to move beyond the assumptions of global flatness
and uniform distance inherent in Euclidean geometry. A Riemannian manifold provides a more powerful and flexible
mathematical framework.
33
A manifold is a space that is locally Euclidean, meaning any small patch of it looks like a flat
plane, but which can have a complex global curvature.
The key to this framework is the Riemannian metric tensor, a function that defines the inner product between tangent
vectors at every point on the manifold. This metric allows for the definition of distance and angles that can vary from
one region of the semantic space to another. The shortest path between two points on the manifold, known as a
geodesic, represents their true semantic distance, which may be very different from their straight-line Euclidean
distance in the embedding space.
35
The curvature of the manifold is a powerful concept for encoding higher-order semantic structures. Different
geometries are suited for different types of data. For example, hyperbolic manifolds, which have constant negative
curvature, are naturally adept at representing hierarchical or tree-like data, as their volume expands exponentially.
36
This makes them ideal for modeling taxonomies or entailment relationships. Conversely, spherical manifolds, with
positive curvature, can model cyclical relationships. This geometric approach provides a principled way to address
polysemy; the different senses of a word can be modeled as occupying distinct regions on the manifold, each potentially
with its own unique local geometry and curvature, allowing for a much richer representation than a single vector can
provide.

suggesting that linear paths in the latent space can be good approximations of geodesics on the generated manifold,
simplifying computation in many cases.

Section 4: Identity by Interface: A Scattering-Theoretic Model of Meaning
This section details the most significant conceptual innovation of the Semantic Field framework: the redefinition of
meaning itself. Moving beyond both static representation and simple gradient descent, it models interpretation as a
dynamic scattering event. The principle of "Identity by Interface" posits that a glyph's meaning is not a point or a vector,
but the complete specification of its interaction with the local semantic field, formalized using the rigorous mathematics
of scattering theory and formal methods.
4.1 The Glyph as a Wave Packet
To capture the inherent ambiguity and potentiality of a word or concept before it is fully contextualized, the framework
models the intruding glyph not as a classical point particle but as a wave packet. In quantum mechanics, a wave packet
is a localized wave disturbance that represents a particle whose position and momentum are not precisely known,
reflecting the uncertainty principle.
19
In the semantic context, the wave packet represents the glyph's initial distribution
of possible meanings. Its spread corresponds to its degree of ambiguity, and its central momentum corresponds to its
initial semantic trajectory.

Section 5: Architecture of a Computational Engine for Semantic Field Simulation

5.1 System Overview
The proposed engine consists of four primary modules, each responsible for a specific phase of the computation. This
modular design promotes clarity, allows for independent development and optimization of each component, and
facilitates experimentation with different algorithms at each stage. The workflow proceeds from raw text data to a final,
verifiable semantic interpretation.

Section 6: Applications, Implications, and Future Trajectories
The Semantic Field framework is not merely an alternative method for generating vector representations; it is a new
paradigm for conceptualizing and computing meaning. Its implications extend beyond traditional NLP tasks, offering new
explanatory power for complex linguistic phenomena and suggesting a path toward more robust, verifiable, and
trustworthy AI systems. This final section explores these implications, contrasts the framework with current state-of-the-
art models, and outlines promising directions for future research.
6.1 Explanatory Power of the Framework
The dynamic, field-theoretic approach provides natural and intuitive explanations for several challenging linguistic
phenomena that are difficult to model with static vector-space semantics.
 Polysemy and Ambiguity: In the Semantic Field, ambiguity is modeled as a glyph being introduced into a region
of the potential landscape that lies between multiple basins of attraction (potential wells), each corresponding
to a different possible meaning. The final interpretive trajectory is highly sensitive to the initial conditions
provided by the wider context, which can subtly nudge the glyph into one basin over another. The problem of
getting stuck in local minima, a known limitation of potential field methods in robotics

, has a direct and
compelling semantic analogue: it represents being led down a "garden path" to an incorrect but locally coherent
interpretation.
 Metaphor and Analogy: Metaphorical understanding can be modeled as a process that goes beyond simple
gradient descent. It may involve trajectories that travel along non-obvious geodesics on the manifold, revealing----------- Page14 ------------

could even be likened to a "tunneling" phenomenon, where a glyph overcomes a potential barrier to move from

 Compositionality: The principle of compositionality—that the meaning of a phrase is a function of the meanings

potential fields generated by each of its constituent glyphs. This creates a complex, composite landscape where
the words mutually constrain each other's interpretation in a non-linear, interactive fashion.
6.2 Comparison with Large Language Models (LLMs)
Current state-of-the-art NLP is dominated by Large Language Models (LLMs) based on the Transformer architecture. It is
crucial to position the Semantic Field framework in relation to these powerful models. The two approaches are not
competitors but are fundamentally different and complementary in their goals and capabilities.
 LLMs are fundamentally generative, predictive models. Their objective function is to predict the next token in a
sequence. They represent a form of intelligence analogous to what psychologists call "System 1" thinking: fast,
intuitive, associative, and pattern-based. Their remarkable fluency comes from having learned the statistical
regularities of a massive amount of text. However, they lack an explicit model of meaning or a mechanism for
rigorous, verifiable reasoning.
 The Semantic Field framework, in contrast, is a model of interpretation and reasoning. It does not predict what
comes next; it computes what something means within a given, structured context. It is an analogue of "System
2" thinking: slow, deliberate, analytical, and verifiable. Its strength lies not in fluency but in its ability to model
the process of satisfying constraints and to have its interpretive outcomes formally proven against a
specification.
The two systems can work in synergy. An LLM could be used to perform the initial "System 1" task of parsing a query and
its context, generating the initial state of the glyph(s) and defining the goal concepts for the potential field. This setup
would then be passed to the Semantic Field engine for a deeper, "System 2" analysis, where the meaning is rigorously
computed and verified.

new information. This would be essential for modeling dynamic phenomena like conversations, evolving belief
states, and learning over time.
 Multi-Glyph Interactions: The current framework focuses on the interaction of a single glyph with a background
field. A more advanced model would treat all entities as glyphs interacting with each other through the field,
akin to a quantum field theory of semantics. This would allow for the modeling of complex, multi-agent
semantic negotiations.
