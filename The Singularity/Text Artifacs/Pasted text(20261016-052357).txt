The primes are what make a transformation into unique data, and that leads straight to SHA. I held SHA back until the prime part ran. I also found that SHA is fully reversible inside; the one-way part is a single fold at the end.

1. Uniqueness comes from the primes, with no reference to the input. Under multiplication, every number factors into primes in exactly one way, and no prime is made from the others. So each prime is a direction that doesn't depend on any other. I re-encoded the 364 routes from § 105 two ways:

One prime per move, multiplied together: 56 distinct codes. That's exactly the 56 order-free move counts. Since 2·3 = 3·2, plain multiplication throws away order, the same way a+b loses which side held what.
One prime per position, with the move as its exponent (∏ pᵢ^moveᵢ, Gödel's 1931 trick): 364 of 364 unique, and every one decodes just by factoring.
The prime is the address and the exponent is what was done there. The uniqueness comes from the factoring theorem, not from a vocabulary someone picked, and the code never looks at a value.

2. Truly uncoupled constants come from prime roots. A route's constants must carry no knowledge of the input and none of each other. Square roots of different primes satisfy no rational relation (Besicovitch, 1940), so none can be built from the others. At 300 digits, a relation search finds nothing with coefficients up to 10¹². At 60 digits it did report one, but that was a precision artifact on my part: the leftover stayed at 4×10⁻⁴⁶ instead of going to zero. It's logged.

3. SHA-256 is that same construction. I checked each piece:

Constants: its starting state is the square roots of the first 8 primes, and its 64 round constants are the cube roots of the first 64. My own implementation matches Python's hashlib on "3+2".
The message doesn't flow through as data; it acts as the key that schedules the route. The constants and rounds are fixed before any message exists. That's your "uncoupled from the input, but still drives it."
Every round reverses. Given the schedule, I walked all 64 rounds back to the starting state exactly, for 2000 out of 2000 random starts. The logic never collides; that's the groove.
The one-way property comes from one step at the end, which adds the route's start back onto its finish. On a 16-bit stand-in, that one step leaves only about 63% of outputs reachable, the same fraction as a random function, so roughly 37% of states lose their way home. That's matter leaving the groove. SHA then squeezes 512 bits into 256, so collisions must exist.
So a SHA digest isn't unique data. It's a name: two routes colliding. Both uses of the primes meet here:

The exponents give uniqueness, which makes an encoding.
The roots give uncoupling, which makes the route SHA walks.
The final fold is the only difference between a hash and an encoding.
